Decoration Day

James M. Barney

OneLovers

Macy and Cole

Mr. Pierson had the lights off and The Breakfast Club going on the projector, which meant he was done teaching for the year and had quit pretending otherwise. Half of homeroom was asleep on folded arms. Hannah sat on Macy’s left with her phone low in her lap, thumbs working, and Macy had the composition book open on her knee under the desk, the marbled black-and-white one she had carried since the fall, her name inside the front cover in marker from when she was thirteen: MACY ELIZABETH TATE, if found please return, i will give you five dollars, and under that, newer and smaller, not five dollars anymore lol.

The book was lists and quotes and one long letter. The quotes page ran in four different pens. Dickinson twice, because of Mr. Pierson. Pound once, for the field mouse, not for Pound. A line from a sermon her aunt had posted, which she loved against her will. A line of Phoebe Bridgers. And one line nobody would recognize, you are the love of my life, which was not from anywhere. It was from Cole, in his car, in February. She had written it down that night without telling him she was writing it down.

The lists page held the trips, every one dated. Sliding Rock the day she got her license. The cutouts with the blanket. His birthday cake riding to Deep Creek in the trunk. The leaves at Maggie Valley, the lights at the bridge, the diner in February, Dollywood because she made him. And at the bottom, in the newest ink, the tunnel twice: once in the daytime with Hannah and them, once at night with just him, just to see.

She had sent him the heart at the start of the hour, the small one. He would still be asleep. He slept on his stomach, one arm under the pillow, one arm thrown out, and she knew that the way she knew her own address. She had typed baby get up under the heart, looked at it a moment, and deleted it. Unsent carried further. She had worked that out on her own and told no one, because telling it would spend it.

Rain was coming tonight, but not till nine. Cole had checked. After school they were driving out, just the two of them, because three weeks ago he had stood behind her on the cutouts with his hands on her shoulders, every finger landing separate, and said they should come back before graduation. She had said they should. He had said just us. She had said just us, and then she had gone home and written it in the book three times so she would have it exactly.

She wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, and was not sure of it, and put i think after it. She kept records now. Every text saved, the snapchats screenshotted where the app allowed it and memorized where it did not. She had not been a person who kept records. Then on New Year’s, in his car in the Methodist church lot with the heater up too high, he had said four words half asleep, and asked her whether he had said them out loud, and she had told him he had, and he had said them again, and then he had said good. That night she wrote the four words on the inside of her arm in pen and washed them off in the morning, and for the hours in between they were there, and she had looked at them. Since that night she had been keeping. She wanted to keep all of it.

A graduation list was going on the facing page. The dress, got. The white shoes, going back. Nails. A gift for Hannah. A gift for Cole, followed by a question mark she had not resolved.

The air through the cracked window was good, early-summer good. She could tell from the first hour what a day was going to be, and this one was going to be good, and she put that down too.

TwoBone Valley

Sydney

The second boat of the morning left the Cable Cove launch at ten of nine, a flat white pontoon in Park Service livery, two rangers aboard and twenty-some of us on the benches with the coolers and the rakes and a casserole riding level in a cardboard box on a woman’s knees, bound across the lake for Bone Valley. Behind us the launch lot sat two-thirds full. North Carolina plates, mostly. Two from Tennessee. One from Georgia, the rental, mine.

A woman in a navy windbreaker, white hair pinned at the nape, had run the dock without raising her voice and without producing a clipboard, moving people and gear onto two boats in an order nobody argued with. She gave me one nod as I came down the gangway, and the nod said I see you, I know what you are, I am not going to make a fuss about it.

The woman on the bench beside me turned out to be cousin to half the boat. She gave me three generations in one sentence: her mother born up at Hazel Creek, lived her life in Bryson City, buried back up the valley we were crossing toward. When I said I would be writing about the day, she looked at the water and then back at me.

“Wear your good listening,” she said.

The lake ran bright and flat the way it does early, before the wind finds it. On the far side a trail crew waited with two pickups and a side-by-side fitted with welded bench seats, Park Service uniforms standing easy in the shade. The gear rode up the old roadbed with whoever needed the ride, and the rest of us walked.

The cemetery lay on a slope behind an iron pipe rail painted black. Inside the rail the stones ran the full range: white government-issue markers, dark granite tablets, hand-cut soapstone, plain fieldstones set on end. One soapstone two rows in carried a name worn nearly out of reading and a date from the 1880s cut clean beneath it. I wrote the year down and meant to come back for the name.

The cleaning went by family group, with no instruction given that I heard. Brushes, rakes, one pump-up sprayer that traveled plot to plot. A black trash bag and a lopper made the rounds for the poison ivy. Children carried water up from the cans by the gate. Nobody hurried and nobody idled.

The chairman was tall, in his sixties, and carried no clipboard either. He moved through the field at a tempo nobody broke. When the cleaning had mostly finished he read Psalm 121 from a leather Bible. “I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help.” The slope went quiet under it, and then “Amazing Grace” started without anyone calling for it. Three verses, then a pause I took for the end, then a fourth. He put the book back in his pocket.

While the tables went up I walked the rows. A woman was working alone at the back corner, kneeling on a folded canvas bag, brushing a stone with her bare hand. A Mason jar she had filled at the dock spigot stood in the grass beside her, and now and then she tipped a little of the water over a stone’s face and brushed it through. Her hands were brown, and a scar ran along the back of one. I asked if I could stand with her a minute, and she allowed it, and the minute became forty.

No name came with anything she told me. She walked me down the row instead. The great-aunt first: married down out of the holler in 1922, came back in a box in ’34. “Diphtheria,” she said, the way you give a road number. Then an aunt by marriage. Then a stone with an 1880s date whose relation to her she let stand unnamed.

“The lake came in ’44,” she said. “We were one of the families.”

Her grandmother had walked out of Proctor with two children and a sewing machine on a borrowed wagon. The government check sat uncashed until somebody made a phone call about it. The grandmother lived in a shotgun house in Bryson City until she died. “What she missed she did not say, but she said it in other ways. You understand.”

The road that was supposed to come back along this shore never came. “Stops at a tunnel,” she said, with no particular weight on it. “The tunnel goes nowhere.” People here call it the broken promise, she told me, or the road to nowhere. Both.

Her mother and her aunt and a few others founded the cemetery association in 1977. “They walked into rooms in Washington and told the truth in plain sentences and came out with a little of what they wanted, every time, never all of it.” Twenty-six cemeteries in the rotation now. Twenty-eight on the original paper from 1943. I asked about the other two.

“The brush has them,” she said.

It landed flat, and it was as close to grief as she was going to come with a stranger standing over her.

“Both of them are gone now,” she said. “My mama and my aunt. The most recent one passed last fall. She would have known the words for today better than the man up there does. He does fine. She would have known them in her sleep.” She moved the brush along the shoulder of the stone. “It is what it is.”

The sentence hung in the air a second and then, without resolving, stopped being relevant.

She was the daughter of one founder and the niece of the other. The two sisters who spent decades riding to Washington were in the coverage I had read before I came down; the more recent of the two deaths was six months old. Neither name was going in my piece.

She offered no meanings. She never said what the day was for, or what I ought to feel about any of it. She brushed lichen off a stone with her bare hand and gave me the history conversationally, because I had asked. She was working. I was visiting. When I thanked her and turned to go, she said, “Drive safely on the road that does not go anywhere.”
